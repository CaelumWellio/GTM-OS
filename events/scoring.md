# How to decide which events to back

This is a method, not a verdict. Events are run by the Head of Marketing and Sales, and the workbook already carries a tier on every row. What follows does not replace that judgement; it gives it six things to check against evidence, and says clearly which of the six have evidence behind them and which do not. Two of the six do not, and I have not dressed them up.

Nothing here produces a score. It produces a band, and the reason is arithmetic rather than modesty: 12 of 151 events have a matched campaign, 9 of those carry a cost, and only 8 have both a cost and a return you can divide one by the other. Eight numbers cannot support a score to two decimals. They can support three bands, and a band is what a booking decision actually needs.

Source: `events/returns.md`; `events/efficiency.md`; `events/data/events.csv`.

## The six dimensions

**One. Audience fit. Has evidence.** Who is in the room decides what a win is worth before anything else does. Secondary wins at 46 percent against 31 percent for primary, at roughly twice the deal size, a median $5,625 against $2,498. Primary lists connect and book at a higher rate, so a primary room is a volume play at half the value, not a bad room. The measured events say the same thing: the primary conference in the set produced 5 wins worth $7,089 in total, about $1,418 a win, under even the primary median. Read the audience field on the event row, and where the workbook has no audience, the research file has attendee roles for 35 of the 50 events it attempted.

Strong is a secondary or K-12 room with principals, deputies and wellbeing leads in it. Mixed is primary, where you should expect more conversations and smaller deals. Weak is a room whose roles nobody has established, because you are then buying a stand on the strength of a name.

**Two. Access through the delegate list. No evidence, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.** The comparison could not be made: of the measured events, 7 had a delegate list and 1 did not, so there is nothing to compare one side against. This dimension earns its place on two other things. The first is a finding. All five delegate list policies the research turned up are opt-in: delegates are asked individually at registration whether sponsors may have their name, school and email, and one organiser states plainly that not every participant is guaranteed to appear on it. So "Delegate List: Yes" in the workbook does not mean the room. It means the share of the room that said yes, and nobody has ever recorded that share. The second is plain logic, not evidence: a stand with no list leaves nothing to follow up on Monday except what someone wrote down.

Strong is a list whose consented count you have seen, on a past event or from the organiser. Mixed is a list promised on the workbook row and never sized. Weak is no list. Almost every AU row sits in the mixed level today, which tells you how little the column is currently worth.

**Three. Voice through a speaking slot. No evidence, and it came out a wash.** Events with a speaking slot averaged 2.94 wins per hundred delegates; events without averaged 2.81, across 5 events and 3. That is not a case for the slot and I will not keep it in the rubric as a quiet positive. It stays in because it is usually the reason a sponsorship package costs what it costs, and because the research file found speaking terms for only 4 of 50 events, so the thing you are paying for is mostly unstated until you ask.

Treat this dimension as a question rather than a credit. If the package with the slot costs materially more than the package without, ask what the difference buys, and put the answer in the event row. The strongest return in the whole set, at 15.2 times, had no speaking slot. That is one event and proves nothing, but it is enough to stop the slot being treated as a requirement.

**Four. Efficiency, as cost per delegate and cost per win. Has evidence, within the eight.** Cost per delegate says what you are paying for access to the room and runs from $1 to $117 across the eight, sitting around $40 to $50 in the middle. Cost per win says what you are paying for the outcome and runs from $325 to $12,500. Cost per win is the one that decides, because it can be read against a number from outside this folder: the 2026 average recorded deal is $7,394.

Strong is under about $1,500 a win, where five of the eight sit. Mixed is between that and $7,394, where two sit. Weak is above $7,394, where one sits, and it is worth stating what that level means rather than softening it: at that point the event costs more per win than the win is recorded at in the year it lands. That is not the same as losing money, because a school licence renews, but it does mean the case has to be made on the second year rather than the first.

**Five. Market need, from state penetration. Has evidence, but it breaks ties rather than deciding.** Penetration by state is NSW 5.7 percent, SA 9.1, QLD 5.4, WA 5.2, VIC 4.5. Four of the five sit inside about a point and a half of each other, so the state on the event row will almost never separate two candidates. Segment is where the range actually is: Catholic combined 30 percent of 132 schools, government combined 10 percent of 535, primary 0.4 percent of 6,156. A primary-audience event is aimed at the emptiest part of the map and the least valuable deal at once, and both of those are true at the same time.

Use this dimension in one direction only. Low penetration in a state means room to sell, not proof that the room wants to buy, and I have no evidence that turns one into the other.

**Six. Measured return, where it exists. Has evidence, with three caveats attached.** Eight events carry a return: 15.2 times, 12.8, 10.6, 6.6, 3.8, 2.3, 1.0 and 0.77. Read this off HubSpot and not off the workbook's performance column, because the two disagree. Across nine events the column overstates wins by about 24. One event claims 8 wins and holds none. Two more claim deals and have no campaign at all, so there is nothing behind the claim to check.

The caveats. A campaign is an assignment made when the deal was created, not proof the event caused it. The cost is the 2026 plan while the return spans 2025 and 2026. And only the Australian pipeline is cached here, so 47 UK and 51 international events have no return at all, which is a gap in what I can see and not a judgement on the events.

Source: `markets/au.md`; `events/returns.md`; `events/efficiency.md`; `events/data/research.csv`.

## The bands

An event does not get a total. It gets a band, set by reading the six together, with the rule that the band is held down by the weakest dimension that has evidence behind it rather than lifted by the strongest.

**Back it.** Audience fit strong, cost per win under about $1,500, and a measured return above 1. Access and voice cannot lift an event into this band, because neither has evidence; they can only add a question to ask before booking.

**Back it, with the condition written down first.** Everything else that has a measured return, plus anything whose fit and need are strong but whose return is not yet known. The condition is one sentence written on the event row before the invoice is paid, naming what the event has to produce for the next booking to be automatic. No condition, no booking: that is the whole discipline, and it costs nothing.

**The case has to come from somewhere else.** Every event with no measured return and no comparable, which today means all 47 UK events, all 51 international ones, and every Australian row with no matched campaign. These are not weak events. They are events this data cannot see, and every one of them moves out of this band the moment a campaign name is recorded against it.

Source: `events/returns.md`; `events/efficiency.md`.

## Two worked examples

**WASSEA, a secondary conference in WA, 200 expected delegates, $9,350.** Audience fit is strong: a secondary room, and secondary wins at 46 percent at roughly twice the primary deal size. Access is recorded as a delegate list, which means the consented share of 200 and not 200, so it sits at mixed like almost every other row. Voice is recorded as a speaking slot, which earns nothing and raises one question: how much of the $9,350 is the slot. Efficiency is strong, $719 a win and $47 a delegate, both comfortably inside the observed range, and $519 per qualified meeting. Market need is middling, WA at 5.2 percent, which is inside the point and a half where this dimension stops separating anything. Measured return is 12.8 times: 20 deals, 13 won, $119,640.

That is five dimensions with nothing pulling it down and one that has no evidence either way. Band: back it. The workbook claims 15 wins where HubSpot holds 13, so the first thing to do is not a decision at all, it is to settle which of those two numbers is right.

**NSWSDPA, a secondary conference in NSW, 400 expected delegates, $25,000.** This is the most expensive line on the 2026 plan and it is the useful example precisely because its top half looks good. Audience fit is strong, a secondary room. Access is recorded as a delegate list. Voice is recorded as a speaking slot. Market need is NSW at 5.7 percent, the same middling level as everything else. Four dimensions in a row give no reason for concern.

The two with evidence behind them do. Efficiency is weak: $12,500 a win, the only event in the set above the $7,394 average deal, and $5,000 per qualified meeting against $160 at the best event in the set. The return is 0.77 times, on 6 deals and 2 wins out of a 400-delegate room, which is 0.5 wins per hundred delegates against 6.5 at WASSEA.

Band: back it with the condition written down first. What the method does here is not tell the Head of Marketing and Sales to drop an event, which would be a judgement on a room I have never stood in. It turns a stark number into three questions that could not be asked before. At the 2026 average deal, $25,000 needs about four wins to come back in year one and this produced two, so what would have to change for four. What does $25,000 buy in a 400-person room that $9,350 does not buy in a 200-person room, and is the difference the room or the package. And the workbook claims 5 wins where HubSpot holds 2, so which of those is the number the decision should be made on. Those are answerable. A tier on a spreadsheet row is not.

## What would change this rubric

Six things, roughly in order of how much they would change it.

A UK pipeline in the cache. Half the workbook is UK and international and none of it can be measured at all, so the rubric currently applies to a third of the events by count and is silent on the rest.

More measured events. Eight returns is what stops this being a score. Every campaign name recorded at booking adds one, and the three events that claim deals with no campaign behind them are the cheapest three to add.

The consented share of a delegate list, recorded once. That single number turns dimension two from logic into evidence, and it decides whether "Delegate List: Yes" is worth anything at all.

One event deliberately run without the thing everyone assumes is necessary. The wash on speaking slots cannot be resolved by more of the same bookings; it needs one comparable event where the slot was declined and the outcome recorded.

A cost figure on every booked event. Three of the twelve measured events have a return and no cost, so they cannot be placed on the efficiency dimension at all.

Evidence that low penetration in a state means unmet need rather than a market that has already said no. Until then, dimension five breaks ties and does nothing else.
