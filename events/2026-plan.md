# The 2026 event plan: what I would change

The Australian event plan commits $149,320 across 22 events that carry a cost. This page says what I would do about it before the invoices are paid. None of what follows is a recommendation to drop an event, because that is a judgement on rooms I have never stood in. All three are things that cost nothing and can be done this term.

Source: `events/data/events.csv`; `events/returns.md`.

## What I recommend

**One. Settle the reconciliation before the three biggest invoices are paid.** The three most expensive lines come to $59,100, 40 percent of the Australian budget, and the workbook and HubSpot disagree on all three. Until someone says which number is right, 40 percent of the budget is being renewed against a figure that has never been checked, because until this folder existed there was no way to check it. This is a morning's work for whoever owns the campaign names, not a project.

**Two. Put a campaign name on the 14 events that carry a cost and have no campaign.** $60,720, 41 percent of the budget, cannot be measured at all, and $24,000 of that sits on events the workbook itself marks tier 1. Nothing about those events is weak. They are invisible, and they stay invisible next year unless a name is written down at the moment the campaign is created. Two are closer than the rest: the national primary principals conference already has two deals sitting in campaigns that match no event row, so it is one line in `events/data/campaign-aliases.csv` away from being measurable, and the Victorian secondary deputies event claims a deal with no campaign at all.

**Three. Ask what $25,000 buys.** One event is 17 percent of the entire Australian budget and returns 0.77 times, on 6 deals and 2 wins out of a room of 400. At the 2026 average recorded deal of $7,394 it needs about four wins to come back in year one and produced two. The question is not whether to keep it. It is what $25,000 buys in a 400-person room that $9,350 does not buy in a 200-person room, and whether the difference is the room or the package.

Source: `events/returns.md`; `events/efficiency.md`; `events/scoring.md`.

## What the money is doing now

The concentration is the finding. Two events, $21,350 between them and 14 percent of the Australian spend, produced $302,108, which is 58 percent of the $525,439 traceable to all twelve matched events. The single best line in the plan costs $12,000 and returns 15.2 times.

Against that, the three most expensive lines return 0.77, 3.79 and 2.29 times. Cost per win across the events that carry one runs from $325 to $12,500, a spread of nearly forty to one, inside a budget currently allocated by tier rather than by return.

| | Spend | Share | Events | What is known |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matched to a campaign | $88,600 | 59% | 8 | seven carry a return, from 0.77x to 15.2x |
| No campaign at all | $60,720 | 41% | 14 | no return measurable |
| Total Australian plan | $149,320 | 100% | 22 | of 53 Australian rows in the workbook |

## What this page does not claim

A campaign is an assignment made when the deal was created, not proof the event caused the deal. The costs are the planned 2026 figures while the returns span 2025 and 2026, so the two are not the same year. The real cost of an event is the sponsorship fee plus the people who worked it, and the workbook holds only the fee, so every figure here understates the true cost by an amount nobody has recorded. And only the Australian pipeline is cached, so 47 UK and 51 international events are absent from this page entirely, which is a limit of what this can see rather than a view on those events.
